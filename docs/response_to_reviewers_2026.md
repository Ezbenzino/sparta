# Response to Reviewers — prepared plan

**Manuscript:** SPARTA: graph structural operators and null models for characterizing spatial tissue architecture in spatial omics

## General response framework

We thank the editor and reviewers for their evaluation. We have revised the manuscript to clarify that SPARTA is a methodological framework for characterizing graph-defined spatial tissue architecture. The revised version does not claim treatment response, prognosis, therapeutic efficacy or clinical utility. We have (i) moved a detailed scope-and-limitations section to the beginning of the Discussion; (ii) corrected the primary melanoma patient mapping and recomputed all patient-level statistics; (iii) added a 50-section public extension cohort spanning Visium and Slide-seqV2; (iv) added cross-cohort robustness, platform and disease-stratified analyses; and (v) expanded structural null, calibration and sensitivity analyses. Specific changes are listed below.

## Anticipated comment 1 — Sample size and public-data heterogeneity

**Anticipated question.** The transcriptomic cohorts are relatively small and combine public datasets generated by different studies. How can the authors exclude heterogeneity, selection bias or false-positive findings due to these differences?

**Response.** We agree that the cohorts are heterogeneous and have revised the manuscript to avoid presenting them as a definitive pooled multi-centre study. The primary, replication and extension cohorts were analysed as separate families, with separate BH correction. The 2026 extension cohort comprised 50 independent public sections from six GEO series and was used specifically for methodological replication. The direction of the field association was consistent across cohorts: 18/19 primary sections, 8/8 replication sections and 48/50 extension sections were positive. The extension patient-level estimate was 0.28 (95% CI 0.23–0.32; sign-test p = 1.08 × 10⁻¹⁰). We have also added a limitations paragraph describing differences in tissue processing, annotations, filtering and library preparation. We interpret these analyses as cross-dataset validation of a structural characterization method, not as a population-level clinical study.

**Supporting material.** Table 1; Table 3; Fig. 4; Fig. 8a,b; Online Resource 1, Tables S12–S13.

**Ready-to-paste text.** We thank the reviewer for highlighting this issue. We do not interpret the combined data as a homogeneous multi-centre cohort. Each cohort was therefore retained as a separate statistical family, and the 50-section extension cohort was used to test whether the direction of the structural association persisted across independent public datasets. The association was positive in 18/19 primary, 8/8 replication and 48/50 extension sections, while the extension patient-level estimate remained positive (0.28, 95% CI 0.23–0.32). We have expanded the limitations to state that processing, annotation, filtering and library-preparation differences preclude definitive pooled epidemiological interpretation.

## Anticipated comment 2 — Slide-seqV2 versus Visium and spatial-resolution effects

**Anticipated question.** Slide-seqV2 and Visium differ substantially in bead/spot size, density and tissue coverage. Are the comparisons valid, and could platform geometry drive the results?

**Response.** We treated the platforms separately and did not reuse Visium spatial parameters for Slide-seqV2. QC was performed at native bead level for Slide-seqV2 and spot level for Visium. For structural analysis, QC-passed Slide-seqV2 counts were summed into fixed 50-µm grid bins, close to the nominal Visium spot diameter, and a platform-specific 100-µm graph radius was used rather than the 150-µm Visium radius. Both platforms retained positive pooled associations (Visium median 0.320; Slide-seqV2 median 0.193). We explicitly report that 50-µm aggregation preserves counts but may smooth small bead-level barriers. The platform comparison is therefore intended to assess qualitative robustness, not exact numerical equivalence.

**Supporting material.** Methods 2.1; Fig. 4a; Fig. 8c; Online Resource 1, Tables S12–S13.

**Ready-to-paste text.** We agree that Visium and Slide-seqV2 require distinct spatial handling. We performed QC at the native spot or bead level and used separate graph radii: 150 µm for Visium and 100 µm for Slide-seqV2 after aggregation of QC-passed beads into fixed 50-µm bins. The positive association persisted on both platforms, although the median effect was smaller for Slide-seqV2. We have added the aggregation procedure and its limitations to the Methods and Discussion, and we interpret the platform comparison as evidence of qualitative robustness rather than exact numerical equivalence.

## Anticipated comment 3 — Correction of primary melanoma patient grouping

**Anticipated question.** The authors corrected the melanoma patient mapping during revision. How did the error arise, which results changed, and how can reviewers verify the correction?

**Response.** The original ledger assigned all four GSE250636 melanoma sections to one patient group. Re-examination of the GEO family SOFT sample characteristics showed that MEL01 and MEL04 belong to GEO patient A, whereas MEL02 and MEL03 belong to GEO patient B. The primary cohort therefore comprises 19 sections from eight, not seven, patients. We implemented the correction in an idempotent script, recomputed section and patient-level statistics, updated Table 1, Fig. 4, the sign-flip test and manuscript text, and prepared a dedicated change log. The sign test for the primary cohort now uses J = 8, with a smallest attainable one-sided p-value of 1/256.

**Supporting material.** `docs/melanoma_mapping_correction.md`; Methods 2.1; Table 1; Fig. 4; scripts/run_54_correct_melanoma_mapping.py.

## Anticipated comment 6 — Plausibility of the null models

**Anticipated question.** Are the graph-spectral and construction null models appropriate? They may impose assumptions about smoothness or preserve too much of the observed structure.

**Response.** We use complementary nulls because no single null answers every question. The graph-spectral surrogate preserves the graph power spectrum while generating fields independent of the observed field, addressing spatial smoothness and dependence. The construction null preserves real ECM inputs while replacing crosslinking and ligand inputs, addressing how much coupling follows from graph construction and shared matrix structure. The geometry null replaces model inputs more broadly. We calibrated the graph-spectral test on real section graphs; pooled false-positive rates were near 0.05 for primary/replication (0.047) and extension (0.042), whereas point-level tests were strongly anti-conservative. We report these nulls as bounds on interpretation rather than proof of a biological mechanism.

**Supporting material.** Methods 2.6–2.9; Fig. 3; Fig. 5; Fig. 8a,f; Online Resource 1, Sections S5–S6.

**Ready-to-paste text.** We designed the null models to answer different questions. Graph-spectral surrogates preserve spatial smoothness and graph dependence; construction nulls retain real ECM inputs while replacing other model components; and geometry nulls replace inputs more broadly. Calibration on real primary and extension graphs showed near-nominal false-positive rates for the graph-aware tests, while point-level tests were anti-conservative. We therefore use the null models to qualify interpretation rather than to claim a unique mechanism.

## Anticipated comment 7 — Batch effects and GEO preprocessing differences

**Anticipated question.** Different GEO datasets may have batch effects and different preprocessing pipelines. Did the authors evaluate this, and could batch structure explain the findings?

**Response.** We agree that batch effects cannot be removed in a retrospective collection of public spatial datasets. We harmonised only the minimum common representation: gene/spatial identifiers, QC, normalisation, signature scoring and graph construction. We did not integrate expression across datasets or remove expression batches, because the goal was not to compare gene-expression levels but to assess whether structural relationships recurred under platform-specific analyses. The QC and metadata tables record dataset, platform, disease stratum, raw/retained dimensions and exclusion reasons. Disease and platform strata are reported separately, and the limitations state that cross-study processing differences remain. A formal batch correction would require shared biological reference samples or paired controls, which are unavailable.

**Supporting material.** Methods 2.1; Fig. 8b,c; Fig. S9; Online Resource 1, Tables S12–S13; extension README.

**Ready-to-paste text.** We agree that conventional batch correction is not feasible because the public datasets lack shared reference samples and differ in tissue processing. We therefore did not pool gene-expression values or estimate cross-dataset expression differences. We harmonised only the structural analysis inputs, retained platform-specific graph parameters, and report disease and platform strata separately. The metadata and QC tables document each dataset and exclusion decision. We interpret the consistent direction as methodological robustness while explicitly retaining cross-study heterogeneity as a limitation.
