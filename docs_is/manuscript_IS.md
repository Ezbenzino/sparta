title: "SPARTA: graph structural operators and null models for characterizing spatial tissue architecture in spatial omics"

# Abstract

Spatial omics maps stromal and immune programmes within tissue, but simple composition summaries do not describe how those programmes are connected. We present SPARTA, a graph-based methodological framework with two structural operators: a minimum-cut operator for graph-defined separation between vessel-proximal immune sources and tumour-nest sinks, and a screened diffusion–absorption field for an in silico molecular-sized probe. Both operators are accompanied by graph-spectral surrogate tests, construction and geometry null models, parameter perturbations and held-out structural comparisons. We analysed 19 primary spatial transcriptomics sections from eight patients, eight independent melanoma sections from four replication patients, and 50 public extension sections from six GEO series, spanning Visium, first-generation Spatial Transcriptomics and Slide-seqV2; patient relationships were unavailable for 11 extension cSCC sections, which were treated as conservative section-level units. The two fields were positively associated in 18 primary sections and 48 extension sections; the extension cohort had a pooled patient-level estimate of {{ext2026_pl_mean}} (95% CI {{ext2026_pl_lo}}–{{ext2026_pl_hi}}). Structural nulls reproduced part of the association, whereas ablating shared matrix inputs reduced but did not eliminate it. In 114 CODEX colorectal-cancer cores from 35 patients, the minimum-cut field was associated with withheld CD8+ T-cell positions. SPARTA characterizes spatial tissue architecture and operator behaviour across platforms; it does not establish treatment response, prognosis, therapeutic efficacy or clinical utility.

**Keywords** Spatial transcriptomics · Multiplexed imaging · Minimum cut · Graph Laplacian · Spatial null model · Tumour microenvironment

# 1 Introduction

Spatial transcriptomics measures gene expression at known positions in a tissue section [1–3]. In tumours, such data are used to map cell states, matrix programmes, immune context and their neighbourhoods [4, 5, 13, 14], and a growing set of methods partitions sections into spatial domains or tests whether labelled cell types co-localise [6–12]. These analyses describe where expression patterns occur. Complementary structural questions concern connectivity between places: whether graph-defined routes connect vessel-proximal immune positions with tumour nests, or how a molecular-sized computational field is attenuated between vessel and tumour-nest nodes.

Transport depends on connectivity as well as on composition. A continuous fibrotic capsule and the same number of scattered fibroblasts have identical composition, but only the capsule separates vessels from the tumour. Graph theory provides operators that are sensitive to this difference. The maximum flow between two node sets equals the capacity of the narrowest separating cut [20], and steady diffusion on a weighted graph is governed by its Laplacian, the discrete analogue of an electrical network [21]. Spot-adjacency graphs of spatial transcriptomics sections are natural substrates for such operators, yet existing graph-based methods in the field address other tasks, such as domain detection [7–10] or cell–cell communication [11].

Posing structural questions on expression-derived graphs raises three statistical difficulties. First, two operators that read the same expression scores are coupled by construction, so an association between their outputs may reflect model structure rather than tissue organisation. Second, spatially smooth fields are autocorrelated, so tests that treat spots as independent observations are anti-conservative [22–24]. Third, sections from the same patient are not independent replicates; counting sections as samples is pseudoreplication [25, 26].

A further difficulty is structural validation: independent measurements of tissue connectivity or cell positions are rarely available together with spatial expression, so a graph summary computed from expression usually cannot be checked against the structural quantity it is meant to describe.

Here we describe SPARTA, a framework that (i) computes a minimum-cut structural operator for graph-defined separation and a screened diffusion–absorption field for a molecular-sized in silico probe on the same spatial graph, with explicit parameters; (ii) tests the association between the resulting fields with a graph-spectral surrogate whose false-positive rate we calibrate on every real section graph; (iii) moves inference to the patient level; (iv) quantifies how much of the observed coupling the model produces by itself, using input-level geometry and construction nulls; (v) produces in-model perturbation maps showing which nodes most affect each operator; and (vi) measures how much of either operator simple summaries of stromal density, distance and neighbourhood composition can reproduce. We examine the cut operator twice: against a process-level benchmark in simulated tissues with planted barriers, and against measured CD8^+^ T-cell positions in multiplexed protein images of colorectal cancer [@schurch2020], where immune cells can be withheld from the operator and used as a held-out structural reference. We apply the framework to 19 primary sections from eight patients with cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) or melanoma, replicate the field association in an independent cohort of four melanoma patients [@thrane2018], and analyse 50 public extension sections, with three small external sections retained as a separate exploratory arm (Fig. 1, Table 1). SPARTA returns model-defined spatial summaries; they are not compared with treatment response, prognosis, therapeutic efficacy or clinical utility, and apart from held-out T-cell positions they are not presented as measurements of molecular exposure or immune-cell passage.

## 2.2 Spatial graph and compartments

Spot coordinates were scaled to micrometres so that the median nearest-neighbour distance equalled the platform pitch (100 μm for Visium, 200 μm for first-generation ST). An undirected radius graph connected spots closer than 150 μm (Visium; six neighbours on the hexagonal lattice) or 300 μm (first-generation ST); edge lengths in μm were stored for distance calculations. Primary graphs had {{spots_min}}–{{spots_max}} nodes and {{comp_min}}–{{comp_max}} connected components; the largest component held {{lcc_min_pct}}–100% of nodes.

Three compartments were defined from rank-normalised signature scores (Section 2.3). Vessel nodes were spots at or above the 80th percentile of the endothelial score. Immune-entry sources were vessel nodes whose mean neighbouring T/NK score was at or above the 60th percentile among vessel nodes. Malignant nodes were at or above the 70th percentile of the tumour-type-specific malignant score, and tumour-core sinks were malignant nodes at or beyond the median graph distance to non-malignant spots. A border-based source fallback for sections with fewer than five vessel nodes was implemented but not triggered.

## 2.3 Expression signatures

Signature scores were computed with Scanpy's `score_genes` (50 control genes, fixed seed) [29] and rank-normalised within each section to $[0,1]$ as $(\mathrm{rank}-1)/(n-1)$. The model inputs were a curated subset of 15 core-matrisome genes (ECM) [30], nine cancer-associated fibroblast markers (CAF), seven crosslinking enzymes (LOX, LOXL1–3, PLOD1, PLOD2, TGM2) [31, 32] and a two-gene ligand score (CD274 and PDCD1LG2, encoding PD-L1 and PD-L2). Compartments used endothelial, T/NK and malignant scores; malignant markers were tumour-type specific (cSCC [4], melanoma [33], epithelial markers for the breast and lymph-node sections). Gene lists and their sources are given in Online Resource 1 (Table S3). These are expression scores, not deconvolved cell proportions or measured protein or matrix concentrations. A score was replaced by the neutral value 0.5 and flagged when fewer than two of its genes passed filtering; this occurred for the ligand score in four primary first-generation sections (CSCC10, CSCC14, CSCC15 and CSCC16) and in {{rep_n_ag_missing_w}} replication sections. In the replication count tables, gene symbols carried appended Ensembl identifiers, which were removed. Because Scanpy was not available where the replication cohort was processed, quality control and scoring were run with a re-implementation of the same steps in NumPy and pandas; re-scoring {{lite_n_sections_w}} primary first-generation sections from their raw tables reproduced the archived signature scores to within {{lite_max_dscore}} and their within-section ranks exactly.

## 2.4 Cellular barrier: source–sink minimum cut

For each edge $(u,v)$, a capacity for cell passage was defined as

$$c_{uv}=\sigma\!\left(a-b\,\bar E_{uv}-c\,\bar F_{uv}\right),\qquad (1)$$

where $\sigma$ is the logistic function, $\bar E_{uv}$ and $\bar F_{uv}$ are the means of the endpoint ECM and CAF scores, and $a=3$, $b=8$, $c=4$. Capacities therefore range from about 0.95 in matrix-poor tissue to below $10^{-4}$ in matrix-rich tissue. The maximum flow between all sources and all sinks (joined to a super-source and super-sink of unlimited capacity) was computed on the largest connected component with NetworkX [34], and the section-level barrier was $B_{\mathrm{cell}}=1/(\text{max flow}+\epsilon)$. By the max-flow/min-cut theorem [20], the same computation returns the minimum cut: the set of edges with the smallest total capacity whose removal separates sources from sinks. These cut edges locate the bottleneck. Capacities were rounded to integer multiples of $2^{-40}$ before the flow computation, because with floating-point capacities the partition read off the residual graph need not be a minimum cut even when the flow value is correct; in our sections the earlier floating-point partition differed from the maximum flow by up to {{mc_old_gap_max_pct}}% in {{mc_n_old_not_min}} of {{mc_n_sections}} sections, whereas the flow values agreed to within $10^{-11}$ (Online Resource 1, Section S14).

For per-spot analyses, the $B_{\mathrm{cell}}$ field was the least cumulative cost of reaching each spot from the sources, with edge cost $1/c_{uv}$ (Dijkstra's algorithm, SciPy [35]). This field is not the minimum cut: a path can bypass a bottleneck at no extra cost if a cheaper detour exists. Spots unreachable from every source received the largest finite value and were flagged.

## 2.5 Molecular-sized screened diffusion–absorption field

Each edge received a conductance for a spherical in silico probe of hydrodynamic radius $r$,

$$g_{uv}=g_0\,e^{-\lambda \bar E_{uv}}\,\Phi(r,\xi_{uv})+g_{\mathrm{floor}},\qquad \xi_{uv}=\xi_0\,e^{-\beta \bar X_{uv}},\qquad (2)$$

where $\bar X_{uv}$ is the mean endpoint crosslinking score and $\Phi(r,\xi)=(1-r/\xi)^2$ for $r<\xi$ and 0 otherwise. $\Phi$ is the steric partition coefficient of a sphere of radius $r$ in a cylindrical pore of radius $\xi$ [36, 37], so $\xi$ is an effective pore radius and is compared directly with the molecular radius. Defaults used an IgG-sized benchmark: $r=5.5$ nm (the hydrodynamic radius of IgG is about 5.3 nm [38]), $\xi_0=20$ nm, $\beta=3$, $\lambda=3$, $g_0=1$ and $g_{\mathrm{floor}}=10^{-6}$. The decay of $g$ with matrix density follows the observation that collagen content can govern interstitial macromolecular diffusion in tumours [15, 16]; no measured drug exposure is used or estimated.

Each node received an absorption rate $\kappa_i=A_i/(A_i+K_{d,\mathrm{eff}})$, with $A_i$ the ligand score and $K_{d,\mathrm{eff}}=0.5$, as a computational ligand-binding term [17, 18]. The steady concentration $\varphi$ solved the screened graph-Poisson system

$$\left(L_g+\mathrm{diag}(\kappa)\right)\varphi=0\ \text{at non-vessel nodes},\qquad \varphi_i=1\ \text{at vessel nodes},\qquad (3)$$

where $L_g$ is the conductance-weighted Laplacian, solved separately in each connected component that contains a vessel. The reported field was $B_{\mathrm{mAb}}=-\log\varphi$. Without absorption, Eq. (3) reduces to a harmonic field whose boundary resistances are effective resistances [21]. Unlike a shortest-path cost, the solution charges a diffusive penalty for detours around absorbing or excluding regions.

Two caveats are built into the definitions. The ligand score encodes PD-L1/PD-L2 expression, not the distribution of PD-1 or of any drug's binding sites, and $K_{d,\mathrm{eff}}$ is uncalibrated. Because $\bar X$ is a within-section rank, $\xi_0$ and $\beta$ fix the range of $\xi$ (from 20 nm at $\bar X=0$ to about 1 nm at $\bar X=1$) and the complete-exclusion threshold $\bar X^*=\ln(\xi_0/r)/\beta={{mesh_threshold}}$; they are qualitative scale parameters, not estimates of tissue mesh size.

## 2.6 Field association and graph-spectral surrogate test

The primary statistic was a partial Spearman correlation between the $B_{\mathrm{cell}}$ and $B_{\mathrm{mAb}}$ fields. Each field was rank-transformed (average ranks for ties), regressed on $[1, z, z^2]$, where $z$ is the standardised rank of the weighted graph distance from each spot to its nearest vessel, and the residuals were correlated with Spearman's ρ. The adjustment removes the shared dependence of both fields on distance from vessels. Point-level p-values treat spots as independent and are reported only descriptively.

Significance was assessed with a graph-spectral surrogate test related to Moran spectral randomisation [22, 23, 39]. The symmetric normalised Laplacian $L=I-D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}$ was eigendecomposed as $V\Lambda V^{\top}$. Surrogates of the $B_{\mathrm{mAb}}$ field $y$ were $y^{*}=V\left(s\odot|V^{\top}y|\right)$, with independent random signs $s_k\in\{-1,+1\}$; they preserve the graph power spectrum of $y$ while randomising its alignment with the $B_{\mathrm{cell}}$ field. With 500 surrogates per section, the one-sided p-value was $(k+1)/501$, where $k$ counts surrogates whose statistic was at least the observed value. Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) false-discovery-rate correction [40] was applied across the 19 primary sections and, separately, across the eight replication sections and the three external sections.

## 2.7 Calibration of the surrogate test on real graphs

To estimate the false-positive rate on each of the {{cal_n_graphs}} primary and external section graphs (and, once the replication cohort had been processed, on its eight graphs), we kept the real $B_{\mathrm{mAb}}$ field $x$ and the real vessel distance as fixed inputs and generated independent random fields $y^{*}=V(|V^{\top}x|\odot e)$, $e\sim N(0,I)$, which share the expected graph power spectrum of the real field but are independent of it by construction. For each graph, {{cal_n_sim}} simulated fields were tested with {{cal_n_sur}} surrogates each, exactly as in Section 2.6, together with the one-sided point-level test (Student's $t$ approximation with $n-2$ degrees of freedom); the same number of fields was tested after the observed marginal distribution of $B_{\mathrm{mAb}}$ had been imposed by rank matching. Two further scenarios used {{cal_n_sim_secondary}} simulations per graph: a strongly skewed monotone transform $\exp(2\tilde y^{*})$ of the standardised field, and heat-kernel fields with spectrum $\exp(-\tau\lambda)$ for $\tau\in\{2,10,50\}$, spanning weak to strong spatial smoothness. Every simulated field was also tested with a normal-score variant of the surrogate test, in which surrogates are drawn from the spectrum of the field's normal scores $\Phi^{-1}((\mathrm{rank}-0.5)/n)$ instead of its raw values; because the association statistic is rank-based, the variant is invariant to monotone transforms of the field. The false-positive rate is the fraction of simulations with $p<0.05$.

## 2.8 Patient-level inference

Section-level tests do not answer whether the association is positive across patients. We therefore used patients as the unit of replication in three ways. (i) An exact sign-flip test of the mean of patient means: under the null hypothesis of a symmetric distribution of patient effects about zero, all $2^J$ sign assignments to the $J$ patient means are equally likely ($J=8$, smallest attainable one-sided p = 1/256). (ii) A nested random-effects model, $r_{ij}=\mu+u_j+w_{ij}+e_{ij}$, for section $i$ of patient $j$, with $u_j\sim N(0,\tau_p^2)$, $w_{ij}\sim N(0,\tau_s^2)$ and $e_{ij}\sim N(0,s_{ij}^2)$, where $s_{ij}$ is the standard deviation of the section's surrogate null distribution, which accounts for spatial autocorrelation. Variance components were estimated by restricted maximum likelihood [41], and inference on $\mu$ used a $t$ reference with $J-1$ degrees of freedom. (iii) Leave-one-patient-out refits. The same procedures were applied to the subsets with a measured ligand score and to cSCC sections only, to the replication cohort ($J=4$; smallest attainable one-sided sign-flip p = 1/16) and, as a secondary analysis, to all 12 patients. The pre-specified replication criterion was a pooled replication-cohort association with a 95% CI excluding zero.

## 2.9 Structural nulls: how much coupling does the model produce by itself?

Three sources can align the two fields after vessel-distance adjustment: anchoring geometry (both fields grow away from overlapping vessel and source sets), model construction (both operators read the ECM score, and the CAF score in Eq. (1) shares five of its nine genes with the ECM set) and the co-arrangement in tissue of the operator-specific inputs (CAF for $B_{\mathrm{cell}}$; crosslinking and ligand for $B_{\mathrm{mAb}}$). We separated them with input-level surrogates that keep each input's graph power spectrum, rank-normalised back to $[0,1]$ exactly like the real scores. The geometry null recomputed $B_{\mathrm{mAb}}$ from surrogates of all three of its inputs (ECM, crosslinking, ligand) while keeping the real $B_{\mathrm{cell}}$ field. The construction null kept the real ECM score in $B_{\mathrm{mAb}}$ and replaced only the crosslinking and ligand scores. Each null used {{dec_n_null}} draws per section; p-values counted draws whose association was at least the observed value. The difference between the observed association and the construction-null mean is the part of the coupling that the model structure alone does not reproduce. As a complementary ablation, the shared ECM term was removed ($b=0$ with $c=12$ in Eq. (1), keeping the total stromal weight, and $\lambda=0$ in Eq. (2)), and the ablated association was re-tested with its own 500-draw surrogate null.

## 2.10 Sensitivity analyses

We recomputed the association with four alternative adjustments for vessel distance (cubic rank polynomial; cubic B-spline of distance in μm with four interior quantile knots; Fisher-pooled Spearman correlations within distance deciles; no adjustment) and after breaking ties in the distance covariate at random. We restricted the association to each section's largest connected component, excluded sections without a measured ligand score, and varied the graph radius (Online Resource 1). Parameter dependence of the size-exclusion law was examined over grids of $\beta$, $\lambda$ and $\xi_0$ (Online Resource 1, Section S1, Table S1 and Fig. S1), and with a permutation control that shuffled the crosslinking score within sections.

## 2.11 Simulation benchmark with a process-level ground truth

We simulated tissues on a Visium-like hexagonal lattice ({{syn_spots}} spots, 100 μm pitch, 150 μm radius graph) with a central tumour nest (radius 500 μm, sinks in its central 300 μm), 14 peripheral vessel sources and matrix-rich spots in {{syn_n_geoms}} geometries: a closed capsule touching the nest; the same capsule with a contiguous angular gap of 5, 10, 20 or 40% of its circumference (removed spots re-placed at random in the outer stroma, so that composition is unchanged); a closed capsule 300 μm further out, separated from the nest by stroma; the same number of spots scattered within a wider peritumoural band; four compact stromal patches; and uniform scatter. Observed scores were rank-normalised sums of the planted pattern, a smooth background and Gaussian noise at three levels (s.d. 0.05, 0.15 and 0.30), with {{syn_reps}} replicates per geometry and noise level ({{syn_n_tissues}} tissues).

The ground truth was a stochastic process distinct from the cut computation: 3,000 agents started at vessel spots and performed a lazy random walk on the true, noise-free tissue, moving from $u$ to a randomly chosen neighbour $v$ with probability $c_{uv}$ from Eq. (1). The fraction of agents reaching the tumour core within 1,500 steps defined ground-truth access. We compared SPARTA's $B_{\mathrm{cell}}$ and its per-spot field (median cost at the core) with six summaries: five computed from the same observed scores (global ECM mean; peritumoural ECM mean within 400 μm of the nest; a neighbourhood-enrichment z-score for contacts between tumour and matrix-rich spots against label permutation, as in Squidpy [12]; the fraction of the tumour boundary covered by a matrix-rich domain from a BANKSY-style clustering of own and neighbour-averaged features [6]; and Ripley's $L$ of matrix-rich spots at 200 μm) and one from geometry alone, the median distance from the vessel sources to the nest boundary. We report Spearman correlations with lost access ($1-$access) and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) for separating closed capsules from 5%-gap capsules, from the equally dense band, and from scatter.

## 2.12 Validation against measured T-cell positions

In the CODEX images, the barrier was computed from the tissue scaffold alone and the positions of CD8^+^ T cells were held out as a structural reference. The protocol was written before any barrier or reference was computed; its three later amendments and the post hoc analyses are listed with it (Online Resource 1, Section S12). Scaffold cells were tumour cells, stroma, smooth muscle, vasculature, lymphatics, adipocytes, nerves and unclassified cells; every immune or mixed immune cluster was withheld and imaging artefacts were removed. In each core, a Delaunay triangulation of scaffold-cell centroids, with edges longer than 50 μm removed, defined the graph, and the analysis used its largest component. The protein analogues of the model inputs were the within-core rank of collagen IV (the only core-matrisome protein measured) for the matrix score and the rank of the mean of the within-core ranks of αSMA and vimentin for the fibroblast score. Capacities followed Eq. (1) with unchanged parameters. Sources were all vascular cells, because T-cell neighbourhoods could not be used to select them, and sinks were tumour cells at or beyond the median hop distance to non-tumour cells, as for the sections. Because cores differ in their number of vessels and in the size of their tumour interface, the primary predictor removed geometry from the cut: $B_{\mathrm{rel}}=F_{\mathrm{open}}/F$, where $F$ is the maximum flow and $F_{\mathrm{open}}$ the maximum flow on the same graph with every capacity set to $\sigma(a)$, that is, matrix-free tissue of identical geometry.

The held-out structural reference was the CD8^+^ infiltration ratio of the tumour core, $\mathrm{IR}=\log_2[(n_{\mathrm{core}}+0.5)/A_{\mathrm{core}}]-\log_2[(n_{\mathrm{rest}}+0.5)/A_{\mathrm{rest}}]$, where every CD8^+^ T cell and every point of a 5-μm tissue raster was assigned to its nearest scaffold cell and the core region was the area assigned to sinks; negative values indicate depletion from the core. Cores were analysed if their graph had at least 100 cells, five sources, 25 tumour cells and ten sinks and if at least ten CD8^+^ T cells were present. The primary test was the one-sided Spearman correlation between patient means of $\log B_{\mathrm{rel}}$ and of IR (Monte Carlo permutation, $10^5$ shuffles); core-level correlations are given with patient-cluster bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (2,000 resamples). Comparators computed from the same scaffold were peritumoural matrix density (the mean of $(8E+4F)/12$ over non-tumour cells within 30 μm of a tumour cell), stromal fraction, a tumour–stroma contact enrichment z-score against 1,000 label permutations, Ripley's $L$ of matrix-rich cells at 50 μm, the absolute $B_{\mathrm{cell}}$ and the $B_{\mathrm{cell}}$ field at the core. Added value was the partial Spearman correlation of $\log B_{\mathrm{rel}}$ with IR given tumour fraction, stromal fraction and peritumoural matrix density (rank-regression residuals). Two geometry-only comparators were added post hoc, after these results had been seen: the median distance from vascular cells to the nearest tumour cell and the median depth of the tumour core (distance from sinks to the nearest non-tumour cell); the added value of $\log B_{\mathrm{rel}}$ was then also estimated given both distances, alone and together with composition, and four alternative weightings of Eq. (1) were examined. Pre-specified secondary analyses tested absolute access ($B_{\mathrm{cell}}$ against CD8^+^ density in the core), whether CD8^+^ T cells are more depleted beyond the SPARTA cut than beyond a geometry-only cut, and differences between CLR and DII patients; five sensitivity variants changed the edge cut-off (30 and 100 μm), used αSMA alone, took all tumour cells as sinks or used all T cells as the outcome.

## 2.13 Counterfactual perturbation experiments and node-sensitivity maps

The gap and size experiments are summarised in Online Resource 1. In the selection-matched gap experiment (S2), 20% of the minimum-cut nodes were set to the 5th percentile of the ECM and CAF scores either as one contiguous arc (best of eight candidate arcs) or as scattered sets (best of eight sets, 100 groups), and the residual $B_{\mathrm{cell}}$ values were compared. The same design was applied to simulated closed capsules of three thicknesses. In the size scan (S3), $B_{\mathrm{mAb}}$ was recomputed for molecular radii from 0.5 to 10 nm with inputs held fixed.

In-model node-sensitivity maps were computed by exhaustive single-spot ablation in all 30 sections: for every spot, the ECM, CAF and crosslinking scores of that spot were set to the section's 5th percentile (the convention of S2; absorption unchanged) and both operators were recomputed. By max-flow/min-cut duality, raising capacities away from a minimum cut cannot raise the maximum flow, so single-spot effects on $B_{\mathrm{cell}}$ are confined to spots on the cut and the informative quantities are joint ones. We therefore ablated the top-ranked 1%, 5% and 10% of the spots of each map together and expressed the reduction as a fraction of a reference: for $B_{\mathrm{cell}}$, ablation of every spot on the cut; for $B_{\mathrm{mAb}}$ (its mean over reachable spots), ablation of every spot. For a budget of 20 spots, each map was compared with the 20 spots of highest local matrix score, 20 random spots of the cut (cellular barrier only), 20 random spots (20 draws each) and the other modality's map; the overlap of the two maps was the Jaccard index of their top 5% of spots. To ask where the high-impact spots lie, each non-malignant spot was assigned to a stromal or an immune compartment by the larger of its mean ECM and CAF score and its mean T/NK, myeloid and B/plasma score; malignant spots formed the tumour compartment (Section 2.2). The share of the top 1% and 5% of each map in each compartment was compared with the share of all spots, pooled over the 29 tumour sections with an exact null for random placement within sections and across the 12 patients with an exact sign-flip test of the mean within-patient difference in shares.

## 2.14 Comparison with simple spatial summaries

To measure how much of each operator could be read off quantities that need no transport model, we computed three summaries in all 30 sections, at spot and at section level. Stromal density was the mean ECM and CAF score over a spot and its graph neighbours (section level: over non-malignant spots adjacent to the malignant compartment). Distance to the tumour was the signed Euclidean distance to the boundary of the malignant compartment, negative inside it (section level: the median distance from vessel spots to the nearest malignant spot). Neighbourhood enrichment, as in Squidpy [12], was the binomial z-score of the number of stromal neighbours of a spot given the section's stromal share (section level: the z-score of tumour–stroma contacts against 1,000 label permutations), with compartments as in Section 2.13. Within sections, each summary was correlated with the $B_{\mathrm{cell}}$ and $B_{\mathrm{mAb}}$ fields (Spearman's ρ). Across the 29 tumour sections, the section-level summaries were correlated with $\log B_{\mathrm{rel}}$, the matrix-dependent part of the cellular barrier defined as for the CODEX cores (Section 2.12), and with the median of each field over tumour-core spots. These comparisons measure redundancy, not accuracy; accuracy was compared where an independent structural reference existed, against simulated access (Section 2.11) and measured CD8^+^ T-cell positions (Section 2.12), with analogues of the same three summaries.

## 2.15 Comparison with spatial-domain methods and runtime

Minimum-cut edges were compared with boundaries between eight spatial domains from a BANKSY-style implementation (own and neighbour-averaged expression features, weight λ = 0.3, k-means) on the same graph [6], scanning the number of domains (4–12) and λ (0.1–0.7). This is not the official BANKSY software, and the comparison describes complementary outputs rather than accuracy. After the exact-cut correction (Section 2.4) the comparison was recomputed with a re-implementation of the same preprocessing in NumPy, pandas and scikit-learn, which, given the earlier cuts, reproduced every archived statistic exactly in {{dom_repro_n}} of {{dom_repro_total}} sections (Online Resource 1, Section S14). Runtimes were measured on one laptop CPU for the SPARTA core operators, the BANKSY-style clustering and Squidpy neighbourhood enrichment [12].

## 2.16 Implementation, reproducibility and use of AI tools

SPARTA is implemented in Python 3.13 with NumPy, SciPy and NetworkX for the operators [34, 35, 42] and Scanpy for scoring [29]; the operator modules import only NumPy, SciPy and NetworkX. All random seeds are fixed and recorded with each output. Per-spot model inputs are exported once to lightweight node tables, from which every statistic in this paper can be recomputed without single-cell libraries; recomputing the primary statistics from the archived node tables on a different operating system reproduced all 19 section-level associations to within {{repro_max_drho}} (differences arise only from floating-point ties) and the $B_{\mathrm{mAb}}$ fields to within $10^{-13}$. The replication cohort was processed without Scanpy (Section 2.3), and cell-resolution graphs use the same operator code. Unit tests cover the operators on synthetic graphs with known answers, the exact minimum cut, the statistical conventions, the cell-graph construction and the Scanpy-free scoring. Large-language-model assistants (Claude, Anthropic; Codex, OpenAI) were used, under the author's direction, to draft and review analysis code, unit tests and figure scripts and to draft and edit the text; every analysis, number and figure was checked against the stored outputs, and the author takes full responsibility for the content.

## 3.1 The minimum-cut barrier recovers planted barrier topology

In simulated tissues, ground-truth access fell from {{syn_access_scattered}} with scattered matrix-rich spots to {{syn_access_closed}} with a closed capsule (Fig. 2b). ## 3.2 The graph-spectral surrogate test is calibrated on real section graphs

On all {{cal_n_graphs}} real graphs, the surrogate test applied to fields that were independent by construction had a false-positive rate of {{cal_pooled_spec}} at α = 0.05 over {{cal_n_tests}} simulations (section range {{cal_spec_min}}–{{cal_spec_max}}, none differing from nominal by a binomial test; Fig. 3a), and its p-values were close to uniform (Fig. 3c). ## 3.4 Most of the coupling is produced by model structure

The structural nulls showed that a substantial part of this coupling is expected from the model alone (Fig. 5). Recomputing $B_{\mathrm{mAb}}$ from spectrally matched surrogates of all its inputs left a small association attributable to anchoring geometry (median geometry-null mean {{dec_geom_median}}); the observed association exceeded this null at p < 0.05 in {{dec_n_p_geom}}/19 sections ({{dec_n_q_geom}} after BH correction). Keeping the real ECM score and randomising only the crosslinking and ligand inputs raised the expected association to a median of {{dec_constr_median}}, a median {{dec_share_constr_pct}}% of the observed value. The ECM and CAF scores were strongly correlated within sections (median Spearman ρ = {{dec_r_ecm_caf}}, range {{dec_r_ecm_caf_min}}–{{dec_r_ecm_caf_max}}), whereas the ECM and crosslinking scores were not ({{dec_r_ecm_xl}}), which explains why the shared matrix input is the main route to coupling by construction.

The excess of the observed association over the construction null was positive in {{dec_n_excess_pos}}/19 sections (median {{dec_excess_median}}) but individually significant in only {{dec_n_p_constr}} ({{dec_n_q_constr}} after BH correction). Pooled across patients, the excess was {{plex_mean}} (95% CI {{plex_lo}}–{{plex_hi}}; p = {{plex_p}}), positive in all seven patients (sign-flip p = {{plex_signflip_p}}), but its leave-one-patient-out p-values reached {{plex_lopo_maxp}}, so this residual signal is modest and fragile. Ablating the shared ECM term reduced the median association from {{abl_median_full}} to {{abl_median}} ({{abl_retained_pct}}% retained); {{abl_npos}}/19 ablated associations were positive and {{abl_n_q05}}/19 remained significant under their own surrogate null after BH correction, with a pooled patient-level estimate of {{plab_mean}} ({{plab_lo}}–{{plab_hi}}). Because CAF and ECM scores overlap in genes and in space, the ablation removes the explicit shared term but not all shared information, which is why the construction null is the more direct measure.

In the replication cohort, the model reproduced less of the coupling. The construction-null mean had a median of {{rep_constr_median}}, a median {{rep_share_constr_pct}}% of the observed association, although ECM and CAF scores were as strongly correlated as in the primary cohort (median ρ = {{rep_r_ecm_caf}}). The excess was positive in all {{rep_n_excess_pos_w}} sections (median {{rep_excess_median}}), exceeded the construction null after BH correction in {{rep_n_q_constr_w}}, and pooled to {{rep_ex_mean}} (95% CI {{rep_ex_lo}}–{{rep_ex_hi}}) across the four patients; with the shared ECM term ablated, {{rep_n_ablated_pos}}/{{rep_n_sections}} associations stayed positive (median {{rep_ablated_median}}; Online Resource 1, Table S7).

In the extension cohort, the construction null reproduced a median {{ext2026_constr_share_pct}}% of the observed association. The median excess over the construction null was {{ext2026_excess_median}}, with a patient-level estimate of {{ext2026_ex_mean}} (95% CI {{ext2026_ex_lo}}–{{ext2026_ex_hi}}; sign-test p = {{ext2026_ex_sign_p}}). Removing the shared ECM term reduced the median association from {{ext2026_median_rho}} to {{ext2026_ablated_median}}, although the ablated association remained positive across most sections and had a positive patient-level estimate ({{ext2026_ab_mean}}, 95% CI {{ext2026_ab_lo}}–{{ext2026_ab_hi}}). Thus, as in the primary and replication cohorts, part but not all of the extension association was attributable to shared matrix inputs.

## 3.5 The size-exclusion term is parameter-driven

Because crosslinking enters as a within-section rank, the size-exclusion law excluded {{mesh_excl_min}}–{{mesh_excl_max}}% of edges in every primary section at the default parameters (Fig. 6a, b), a narrow range across two platforms and a 26-fold range of sequencing depth that reflects the fixed threshold $\bar X^*={{mesh_threshold}}$ rather than tissue biology. The excluded fraction varied steeply with $\beta$. In a variance-ablation analysis, the crosslink/size channel accounted for a median {{xl_share_real}}% of $B_{\mathrm{mAb}}$ variance at $\beta=3$, and shuffling the crosslinking score within sections gave the same share ({{xl_share_null}}%), confirming that this percentage is a property of the rank-based law, not a measurement. The mean tumour-core $B_{\mathrm{mAb}}$ increased monotonically with the specified molecular radius in {{s3_n_monotone}}/19 sections (median {{s3_ratio_median}}-fold from 0.5 to 5.5 nm; Fig. 6c), as the programmed law requires; this demonstrates operator behaviour, not molecular exposure.

## 3.6 External sections, counterfactual gaps and comparison with domain methods

In the external arm, all three associations were positive ({{ext_rhos}}; BH q = {{ext_qs}}) and each exceeded its construction null; the two breast sections come from one patient and the lymph node is non-tumour tissue, so these results show that the calculation transfers across datasets, not that a tumour barrier is validated (Online Resource 1). Together with the replication cohort (Section 3.3), the association was positive in all 11 sections from the six patients analysed outside the primary cohort with the unchanged pipeline.

In the selection-matched gap experiment, residual $B_{\mathrm{cell}}$ after scattered removal exceeded that after contiguous removal by a median factor of {{s2_median}} (range {{s2_min}}–{{s2_max}}; {{s2_n_q05}}/19 sections BH q < 0.05; pooled patient-level ratio {{pls2_ratio}}, 95% CI {{pls2_ratio_lo}}–{{pls2_ratio_hi}}; Fig. 6d). In simulated closed capsules, where a continuous barrier is known to exist, the same matched design gave ratios scattered around 1 (medians {{s2thick_med_min}}–{{s2thick_med_max}} for capsules 160–500 μm thick, {{s2thick_nsig}} of {{s2thick_n}} replicates at p < 0.05, Fig. 6d; median {{syn_s2_median}} in the main benchmark, Online Resource 1, Section S5). S2 therefore does not test barrier continuity, and we do not use it as evidence of topology.

Minimum-cut edges were enriched among BANKSY-style domain boundaries (median {{dom_enrich_median}}-fold, range {{dom_enrich_min}}–{{dom_enrich_max}}), but only {{dom_prec_median_pct}}% of boundary edges were cut edges, and these figures were stable across neighbourhood weights (median enrichment {{dom_lambda_enrich_min}}–{{dom_lambda_enrich_max}}) and numbers of domains (Online Resource 1). Domains and cuts are complementary outputs: a domain boundary marks a change in composition, whereas a cut marks where capacity limits transport. The SPARTA core operators ran in a median {{rt_sparta_median_s}} s per section ({{rt_sparta_total_s}} s for all 19 sections) with a peak memory below {{rt_peak_mem_mb}} MB on a laptop CPU.

## 3.7 The minimum cut tracks measured CD8^+^ T-cell exclusion in multiplexed images

Of the {{cx_n_cores_total}} cores, {{cx_n_cores}} from all {{cx_n_patients}} patients met the inclusion criteria (median {{cx_lcc_median}} scaffold cells and {{cx_cd8_median}} CD8^+^ T cells per core; exclusions in Online Resource 1, Section S12). CD8^+^ T cells were depleted from tumour cores in most cores (median IR = {{cx_ir_median}}), and higher geometry-normalised barriers went with stronger depletion (Fig. 7a, b): the patient-level correlation between $\log B_{\mathrm{rel}}$ and IR was {{cx_rho_pat}} (one-sided p = {{cx_p_pat}}; core level {{cx_rho_core}}, 95% CI {{cx_ci_lo}} to {{cx_ci_hi}}). The direction held in all {{cx_sens_pre_n_w}} pre-specified sensitivity variants (patient-level ρ from {{cx_sens_pre_rho_max}} to {{cx_sens_pre_rho_min}}, all one-sided p ≤ {{cx_sens_pre_pmax}}) and in {{cx_sens_ph_n_w}} post hoc re-weightings of Eq. (1), including matrix-only and fibroblast-only capacities ({{cx_sens_ph_rho_max}} to {{cx_sens_ph_rho_min}}), and the absolute $B_{\mathrm{cell}}$ ({{cx_bcell_rho_pat}}) and the field at the core ({{cx_field_rho_pat}}) behaved similarly.

The barrier was not uniquely informative (Fig. 7c). Peritumoural matrix density was as strongly associated with depletion ({{cx_peri_rho_pat}}), and tumour–stroma intermixing was strongly associated in the opposite direction ({{cx_contact_rho_pat}}): cores whose tumour cells were interspersed with stroma had more CD8^+^ T cells in their tumour cores. Of two geometry-only summaries added post hoc, the distance from vascular to tumour cells was uninformative ({{cx_dist_rho_pat}}), whereas the depth of the tumour core was the strongest single correlate ({{cx_depth_rho_pat}}), partly by construction, because the outcome is measured in that core. # 4 Discussion

## 4.1 Scope and limitations

This study is a methodological exploration of graph-defined spatial tissue architecture. The operators summarize connectivity, separation and field attenuation in expression-derived spatial graphs; they are not estimates of treatment response, prognosis, therapeutic efficacy, drug delivery or clinical utility.

Several limitations follow from that scope. All graph parameters are qualitative and were chosen for computational comparability. Signature scores are proxies for cell states and matrix content, not measured cell proportions, protein concentrations, collagen mechanics or pore sizes. The ligand score represents PD-L1/PD-L2 expression rather than drug-specific binding, and the molecular-sized diffusion field has not been calibrated against measured molecular exposure. The only direct biological comparison is to static CD8^+^ T-cell positions in one CODEX data type; a snapshot reflects recruitment, retention and death as well as movement, and it cannot validate the diffusion–absorption operator.

The transcriptomics evidence is also limited by sample size, public-data heterogeneity and platform differences. Before the extension cohort, the transcriptomic comparisons comprised 27 sections from 12 patients across three studies and two platform generations. Treatment history and paired histology were often unavailable, compartments were inferred from expression rather than pathology, and graph disconnection stranded some sources and sinks in eight primary sections (at most {{max_stranded_pct}}% in {{max_stranded_section}}). The melanoma patient mapping in the primary cohort was corrected during this work from one to two patients; the correction, evidence and all recomputed statistics are recorded in a dedicated change log. The external arm is small and includes adjacent sections from one patient plus a non-tumour lymph node. In the CODEX analysis, geometry-only comparators and intermixing adjustment were added after the pre-specified results had been seen. The domain comparison used a BANKSY-style implementation rather than the official software, and runtimes are machine-specific.

The 2026 extension cohort increases the number of transcriptomics sections to 77 but does not remove these limitations. The extension data were generated by independent studies with different tissue-processing protocols, reference annotations, filtering choices and library preparations, and no raw clinical metadata beyond the public GEO records were harmonised. Eleven cSCC sections in GSE289745 lacked patient identifiers; although they were treated as conservative section-level sampling units, their true dependence structure is unknown. Slide-seqV2 provides near-cellular bead measurements, but the structural analysis summed QC-passed beads into 50-µm bins to control graph size and make the spatial scale comparable to Visium. This aggregation preserves counts but cannot recover bead-level topology and may smooth small barriers. Conversely, the Visium and Slide-seqV2 radii were necessarily platform-specific, and residual differences in node density and tissue coverage can affect both effect size and graph connectivity. These analyses should therefore be interpreted as cross-dataset methodological validation, not as a pooled multi-centre study.

## 4.2 Interpretation of the structural operators

SPARTA turns expression maps into answers to two graph-structured questions: where the narrowest separation between vessel-proximal immune sources and tumour-nest sinks lies, and how a molecular-sized computational field is attenuated by dense, crosslinked matrix and computational ligand absorption. In simulations, the cut operator responded to barrier continuity rather than composition and distinguished closed from gapped capsules where composition, distance, domain coverage and neighbourhood statistics did not. In multiplexed images, where T cells could be withheld and used as a held-out structural reference, the cut computed from collagen IV and αSMA/vimentin alone was associated with measured CD8^+^ T-cell depletion from tumour cores across 35 patients. The operator therefore captures a structural correlate of T-cell exclusion, not only a property of the model that defines it.

The same comparison sets its limits. Peritumoural matrix density was associated with depletion as strongly as the cut; tumour–stroma intermixing and, in a post hoc comparison, tumour-core depth were more strongly associated; and the cut's added value persisted after adjustment for composition and geometry but shrank once intermixing was accounted for. The cut was also not the observed line of T-cell arrest: it summarizes a tissue's structural association with exclusion rather than locating the T-cell front. Stronger tests would require time-resolved or functional measurements; the diffusion operator has no such test in this study.

Across 27 transcriptomics sections from 12 patients, the two model fields were positively associated within sections and in every patient. The structural nulls change how this association should be read. In the primary cohort, shared anchoring geometry and, above all, the shared matrix input reproduced a median {{dec_share_constr_pct}}% of the coupling, and the residual excess was small and fragile. In the melanoma lymph-node metastases of the replication cohort, the model reproduced a smaller share ({{rep_share_constr_pct}}%) and the excess was positive in all four patients. The two fields thus co-vary mainly because both are driven by stromal matrix programmes, with additional alignment of diffusion-specific inputs whose size differed between cohorts; with four and eight patients in the relevant melanoma groups, we cannot determine whether tumour type, metastatic site or platform explains the difference. Construction nulls are essential for interpreting such comparisons.

Adding the extension cohort reproduced the principal methodological finding under heterogeneous public data: 48 of 50 external sections had a positive association, with a pooled estimate of {{ext2026_pl_mean}} (95% CI {{ext2026_pl_lo}}–{{ext2026_pl_hi}}). The direction was consistent across cSCC and metastatic melanoma, across Visium and binned Slide-seqV2, and after removing the shared ECM term; the smaller primary-melanoma stratum remained uncertain. This pattern supports the robustness of the graph-based characterization while also showing that effect magnitude depends on tumour subtype, tissue coverage and platform.

Simple spatial summaries are inexpensive and useful, making it important to quantify what the graph operators add. Within sections, local stromal density shared about a quarter of the rank variance of the cellular field and less of the molecular field; distance to the tumour and neighbourhood enrichment shared even less, and the section-level cellular operator was unrelated to all three. The operators add connectivity: equal density and equal contacts can hide or reveal a continuous passage, which only the cut detected in simulations, and in the CODEX cores the cut carried information about T-cell depletion beyond composition and geometry even though it was no better than the best single summary. The operators also return quantities that summaries do not provide: the location and capacity of a bottleneck, node sensitivity under specified perturbations, and a diffusion field for a probe of a given size. Whether these quantities correspond to real tissue processes can only be settled by independent measurements.

The in-model perturbation maps illustrate the difference between the operators. The cellular operator was node-sensitive: in the model, the single most effective node changed up to {{iv_single_cell_max}}% of the minimum-cut value, and the top 1% of nodes changed a median {{iv_top1_cell_median}}%, because a minimum cut has a finite set of bottleneck edges. The molecular field was less focal: the same node budget changed {{iv_top1_mab_median}}% of the reference and even the top 5% of nodes only {{iv_top5_mab_median}}%, because the diffusion field depends on many edges. High-sensitivity nodes of the two operators hardly overlapped (Jaccard index of their top 5% of nodes, median {{iv_jacc_median_pct}}%, range {{iv_jacc_min_pct}}–{{iv_jacc_max_pct}}% across primary sections), were not simply the densest matrix positions, and were distributed across tumour, stromal and immune compartments. These maps describe computational sensitivity only; they do not identify clinical interventions.

Three statistical lessons generalize beyond this application. First, spot-level tests between smooth fields are severely anti-conservative on real section graphs (false-positive rates of {{cal_naive_min}}–{{cal_naive_max}} at a nominal 0.05), whereas graph-spectral surrogates are calibrated when normal-score surrogates are used for strongly skewed fields. Second, nested designs in which several sections come from the same patient require patient-level inference; here patient-level conclusions agreed with section-level ones and replicated in an independent cohort, but they rest on a limited number of patients. Third, feeding within-section ranks into physically motivated laws produces outputs that appear measurement-like, such as the 60–65% excluded edges or the high crosslink variance share, but are fixed largely by parameter choices and should be reported as model properties.

Useful future work is therefore validation rather than clinical extrapolation. Calibrating ξ against collagen imaging such as second-harmonic generation, and κ against target protein density, would replace qualitative parameters with measured ones. Cell-resolution transcriptomic platforms could reduce mixed-state inputs. Most importantly, paired measurements of molecular distribution or time-resolved T-cell infiltration in the same tissue would be needed to test whether the computational fields correspond to real processes. Until then, SPARTA is best interpreted as an analytical and hypothesis-generating framework for spatial architecture, with outputs read together with their structural nulls.

# 5 Conclusions

SPARTA provides two complementary graph structural operators for spatial omics, a minimum cut for graph-defined cellular separation and a screened diffusion–absorption field for a molecular-sized in silico probe. Across 77 transcriptomics sections from primary, replication and heterogeneous public extension cohorts, the operators showed reproducible spatial associations that were partly explained by construction and partly reduced by removal of shared matrix inputs. The minimum cut also aligned with withheld CD8+ T-cell positions in independent CODEX images. These results support SPARTA as a reproducible framework for characterizing spatial tissue architecture and comparing structural hypotheses; they do not establish treatment response, prognosis, therapeutic efficacy or clinical utility.
